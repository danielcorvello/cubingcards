History of the methods

Where each method comes from and who created it, puzzle by puzzle.

3x3

The Rubik's Cube

Hungarian Ernő Rubik, a professor of architecture and design in Budapest, created the cube in 1974 to show his students how pieces can turn without the whole thing falling apart, and it took him about a month to solve his own cube for the first time. Patented in Hungary as the "Magic Cube", it reached the shops of Budapest in 1977; in 1980 Ideal Toy took the cube to the world under the name Rubik's Cube, and it became one of the best-selling toys in history. In 1982 Budapest hosted the first world championship, won by American Minh Thai with 22.95 seconds. The craze faded, but the internet brought the fast methods back in the late 1990s; in 2004 the World Cube Association (WCA) was born, and it runs the official competitions to this day, where the fastest solve the 3x3 in under 5 seconds.

  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cube_Association

The beginner method (layers)

Solving in layers (cross, 1st layer corners, middle edges and the last layer in a few steps) is the oldest idea: the first known publication is by Donald Taylor, in 1978. David Singmaster, who started out solving the corners first, put together his layer version between 1979 and 1980 in the "Notes on Rubik's Magic Cube", and James Nourse's book (1981), one of the best sellers of that year, took the method to the general public. It is almost everyone's path to this day.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Methods/LayerByLayer
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layer-by-layer_method

CFOP (Fridrich)

CFOP = Cross, F2L, OLL, PLL. It doesn't have a single inventor. The cross already appeared in 1978 (Taylor); F2L, pair by pair, was published by John Conway, David Seal and David Benson in 1979 and by several Dutch cubers in 1981. OLL and PLL (the last layer in two steps) came up at the same time in 1981: Hans Dockhorn and Anneke Treep in the Netherlands, and Jessica Fridrich with Mirek Goljan in what was then Czechoslovakia; the letter names of the PLLs (T, J, Y…) come from him. Fridrich put it all together, completed the algorithms and published it on the internet in 1997; that's why the method became known as "Fridrich", and it is the most used in the world.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Methods/CFOP
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFOP_method
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessica_Fridrich

OLLCP

Doing OLL while also putting the corners in place was proposed by Masayuki Akimoto and others in the early 2000s (first known mention in 2005). Kenneth Gustavsson published the full list in 2006 under the name XCLL; the community started calling it OLLCP around 2010.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Steps/OLLCP

Roux

Created by Frenchman Gilles Roux, who got back into cubing in 2001 and wanted an almost fully intuitive method, finished with just M and U. The idea: build two 1×2×3 blocks on the sides (leaving the middle slice free), solve the top corners (CMLL) and finally the last 6 edges (LSE). He presented the method to the community in August 2003; the name CMLL is his (2005). It is known for using few moves and almost no rotations. Roux was also on the WCA board (2004–2008).

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Methods/Roux
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Roux

Last slot: WV, SV and VLS

Ideas for orienting the last layer while inserting the last pair, skipping OLL. The Winter Variation was proposed by Lucas Winter in January 2005; the Summer Variation, by Chester Lian in 2009. Mats Valk had used a larger set since 2009, for the pair ready in any shape, without publishing it; in 2013, Rowe Hessler came to the same idea, and the two split it: VLS (Valk Last Slot) for the pair already built and HLS for the pair one R U R' away from ready. Valk used a VLS algorithm in his 4.74 s world record, in 2016.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Steps/OLS
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mats_Valk

ZB and ZBLL

In 1981, Lars Petrus proposed orienting the edges first, so the last layer could be done in a single step. Around 2000–2001, Bernard Helmstetter generated the algorithms for these cases, and Zbigniew Zborowski and Ron van Bruchem brought the idea to CFOP (the ZB method: ZBLS orients the edges on the last pair; ZBLL solves the last layer). The name ZBLL appeared in 2005. With 472 cases, for a long time it was something for few; today several of the fastest in the world use it.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Steps/ZBLL
  • https://cubinghistory.com/3x3/Steps/ZBLS

2x2

The 2x2

The 2x2 came before the Rubik's Cube: American Larry Nichols invented a 2×2×2 held together by magnets in 1970, patented in 1972. Years later, the company that owned the patent sued the maker of the Rubik's Cube, and the court (1986) ruled in its favor in the case of the 2x2.

Sources
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_Cube

CLL and EG

CLL (solving the top layer at once with the bottom one done) had been discussed since 2003; the first site with algorithms made for the 2x2 was Erik Akkersdijk's, in 2006. EG goes further: with only the bottom face done, it solves everything at once. Łukasz Ciałoń was already solving this way in 2005; Gunnar Krig proposed the idea in 2006 and Akkersdijk published the EG-1 algorithms; the name comes from Erik and Gunnar. The EG-2 ones were generated by David Woner in 2008. It is the method of the fastest 2x2 solvers.

Sources
  • https://cubinghistory.com/2x2/Methods/EG
  • https://cubinghistory.com/2x2/Methods/CLL

Guimond

The method is by Gaétan Guimond, a cuber from Montreal, Canada, who says on his website that he created it in 1982. The idea is the one behind corners-first methods: twist the corners, separate the two colors and only then put each one in place. It caught on because it uses few turns (about 16) and because separation can be predicted during inspection. The last step is the same PBL as Ortega; in 2004, Guimond also proposed a version for the 3x3.

Sources
  • https://www.cubinghistory.com/2x2/Methods/Guimond
  • https://www.speedsolving.com/wiki/index.php/Guimond_Method

Ortega

The method is named after Victor Ortega, who spread it (in 2001 it went up on Josef Jelinek's site), but the idea is older: it comes from a corners-first 3x3 method, published by Jeffrey Varasano in 1981, which is why it is also called the Varasano method. It is the next step for those who solve the 2x2 by layers: face, OLL and PBL, with about 12 algorithms.

Sources
  • https://speedsolving.com/wiki/index.php/Ortega_Method
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_Cube

4x4

The 4x4 and reduction

The 4x4 (Rubik's Revenge) was created by Péter Sebestény and released in 1981. Since it has no fixed centers, the most common method is reduction: build the centers, pair up the edges and finish like a 3x3. But the 4x4 allows swaps that don't exist on the 3x3, hence the parities (OLL Parity and PLL Parity). The Yau method, by Robert Yau (2009), is a reduction in a different order (centers and cross before the edges).

Sources
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubik%27s_Revenge
  • https://www.cubeskills.com/uploads/pdf/tutorials/intermediate-tips-and-yau-method.pdf

5x5

The 5x5 and reduction

The 5x5 (Professor's Cube) was created by Udo Krell in 1981 and made by Uwe Mèffert from 1983. It is also solved by reduction: centers, edges (the 3 pieces of each one) and then like a 3x3. Unlike the 4x4, once reduced it has no parity: the hard cases come when pairing the last edges (L2E).

Sources
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor%27s_Cube

Megaminx

Megaminx

The puzzle dodecahedron was invented by several people independently; Uwe Mèffert bought the rights to some of the patents and sells it under the name Megaminx. It has been an official WCA event since 2003. It is solved like a longer 3x3: a "star" of edges (like the cross), the layers around it and the last face with algorithms, in 4 steps (EO, CO, EP, CP, as in the tutorial by Feliks Zemdegs and Andy Klise) or in two (OLL and PLL).

Sources
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaminx
  • https://www.cubeskills.com/uploads/pdf/tutorials/intermediate-megaminx-techniques.pdf

Pyraminx

Pyraminx

The tetrahedron was created and patented by Uwe Mèffert and released by the Japanese company Tomy in 1981, at the height of the Rubik's Cube craze. Speedsolving methods start with the tips and then build a "V" (two edges and the centers) or a whole layer, as in Top-first: hence L4E (the last 4 edges) and L3E (the last 3).

Sources
  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyraminx

Skewb

Skewb

The Skewb was invented by Tony Durham and released by Uwe Mèffert around 1982, first as the "Pyraminx Cube"; the name Skewb came from Douglas Hofstadter, in his column about the Rubik's Cube. Unlike cubes, it turns around its corners: each turn moves half of the puzzle. It became an official WCA event in 2014. Sarah Strong's method, with the sledgehammer and the hedgeslammer, is almost everyone's path: Intermediate has 10 cases, and Advanced solves the last two layers in one go.

Sources

  • https://www.worldcubeassociation.org/posts/skewb-new-event-in-2014
  • https://sarah.cubing.net/skewb/my-method
